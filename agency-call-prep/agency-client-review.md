# Supplement Brand agency: client site review and call prep

Prepared 11 Nov 2026 for the Wednesday call. All revenue figures are the agency's own claims and haven't been verified. Prices are as listed on each site on 11 Nov.

## The short version

- **Who they are.** The agency is **Supplement Brand** (supplementbrand.co), co-founded by **Lou Antonio**. Lou built Youth Sport Nutrition from 2017, went on Dragons' Den, and was featured on Shopify Masters. They teach the same order every time: customer, then offer, then page, then ads. The front door is a free community, a free 45-minute call and a profit calculator. No paid pricing is shown, and the footer says "results vary and are not guaranteed".
- **What the portfolio shows.** Nearly every brand is a **single hero product for a very specific person**: shift workers, rideshare drivers, thru-hikers, women on GLP-1 drugs, women in perimenopause, Ninja Creami owners, kids who skip breakfast. Almost none is a general "sports supplement" range.
- **The format is the hook.** Most brands use a familiar ingredient in a new format, such as strips, gummies, honey sachets, coffee or ice-cream mix. **Creatine comes up eight times** in a new format: Pumpy, Nootra, H3O, Code Nutra, ByEla, Mareshi, ODED and NextGen. That's the most direct read-across for a sports brand.
- **The playbook is the same on every site.** A long-form product page, a 30 to 365-day money-back guarantee, 2 and 3-pack price breaks, subscribe and save at 15 to 30%, press logos, UGC video, and a week-by-week results timeline.
- **Price band.** A one-month supply is mostly **$40 to $80, £25 to £70 or €40 to €75**. Bundles push the order value to roughly $100 to $180.
- **Quality flags.** Several sites have messy details: copy from another product left on the page, compare-at prices lower than the sale price, conflicting guarantee lengths, unsourced "91% report…" stats, placeholder lorem ipsum, and reviews that look duplicated. One brand (Camden) appears to be white-label. A few "clients" are pre-launch. That fits a fast-launch, test-and-iterate model, but it's worth knowing.

## The video

**"Lou Antonio: The Mindset Required to Make $1M in 2025"** on Darren Lee's channel ("Kickoff Sessions"), uploaded 18 Aug 2024, about 2 hours 9 minutes. This section is based on the full transcript you uploaded. It's a friendly chat between two coaches rather than a tough interview, so his numbers go unchallenged and should be treated as his claims.

**Where the substance is:** roughly 23:00 to 1:25:00. The first 23 minutes are his upbringing, and the last 45 cover his routine, relationships and moving to Bali.

### His origin story (32:36–41:00)
- **The idea.** While working at Newcastle United's academy, he saw kids eating junk between school and training. That led to a healthy powder shake for young athletes (Youth Sport Nutrition).
- **The mistake.** He spent **two years building the product** without selling anything, while carrying £15–30k of personal debt. **The launch produced one sale**, from a friend who wasn't even in the target market.
- **The recovery.** For 1.5 to 2 years he drove around the UK every weekend to sports clubs, swim meets and football training, handing out free samples. It cost more than it earned, but it gave him real objections from parents. He moved those answers onto the website and put buyers on a **subscription**. Once the subscriber base was big enough, he moved fully online.
- **His lesson.** Being first in a "blue ocean" with no competitors was **a hindrance, not an advantage**, because he had to educate the market himself.
- **Where it ended up.** He says the brand makes **"probably 50–60k a month"** (elsewhere "40–50–60k") on about 2 hours of his time a week. He also says that four years in he still wasn't taking a salary from it.
- **Funding.** An investor (the father of a friend of a friend) gave him **£10k for 15%** (asking 20%). Later top-ups from the same investor were **released in stages against revenue targets**.
- **Manufacturing.** About £4–5k of the £10k went on the first production run. He **phoned 52 contract manufacturers and 50 said no.** A small, start-up manufacturer took a chance on him and still makes the product. The first batch was only about 50 units per flavour.

### Validation advice (opening, 25:00–26:13, 35:40–36:31)
- **"Tell everyone about it, validate it, if they come then build it."** Don't build first.
- **Look for objections, not confirmation.** Objections are the data you either overcome or pivot around. Get them from real potential customers before spending money.
- **Use what you have.** Ask your 400 Instagram followers or 20 email subscribers. Some silence on a poll beats silence after four months of building.
- **Mindset he recommends:** a constant "hypothesis, test, do more of what works" loop, which he credits to *Lean Startup*. He also says the only real shortcut is paying people one or two steps ahead of you, which is a pitch for coaching.

### Unit economics (52:14–56:43)
- **His first numbers were broken.** He sold at **£29.30** and paid **£17–18** a unit, because tiny minimum order quantities meant no bulk discount. Then he ran **20–45% launch discounts** to get any sales at all. His operations-minded business partner made him stop.
- **The constraint.** They only had cash for one batch, so the batch had to sell at break-even or better to fund the next order.
- **His worked example of why margins disappear.** Buy for 10 and sell for 20, and you seem to make 10. Then take off 20% VAT, operating and software costs, and paying yourself. **Then the cost of acquiring the customer, "the biggest thing no one thinks about"**, and you're in the red before you start.
- **Later problems were stock, not demand.** Manufacturer lead times meant they sold out faster than they could restock. They needed to order bigger batches to improve the margin.
- He claims **9 out of 10 founders who come to him can't read a profit and loss statement or a balance sheet.**

### Cash flow and retention (56:43–1:02:25)
- **The first two things he does with any client** are a deep dive on the **customer avatar** and a **cash flow spreadsheet**. The spreadsheet covers product cost, operating costs, tax and the founder's pay. He gives it away free in his Skool community, which is likely the "profit calculator" on the agency site.
- **The host's cautionary tale.** Buying a £2k box of vintage stock, selling it for £5k, and spending £3k on ads leaves you where you started.
- **His "eternal funnel."** Most e-commerce focuses only on acquisition. Instead, keep the customers you win: subscriptions, consumables that run out, "superior unexpected customer service" and automated follow-ups that turn buyers into advocates. **Unless your margins are very high, acquisition alone won't scale.**
- **Repeat purchase is why supplements appeal to him.** They run out, so recurring revenue is built in, unlike a one-off product such as a massage gun.

### Building a brand (1:02:25–1:10:52)
- **Start with a product that solves a real problem**, not a fad.
- **Once you have product-market fit**, go back to the supplier to improve the formula and make it your own. That gives you a point of difference and a moat. This matches the "moved to a custom formula" notes on Meltem and Crushs.
- **Grow by selling related products** to existing customers, who trust you more than an unknown brand.
- **The exit** is selling the brand to a bigger company that bolts on your cash flow.
- **The catch.** Starting has never been cheaper (Shopify, AI-driven Meta ads, free YouTube advice), so **competition has never been higher.** A clear point of difference is essential.

### Influencers and partnerships (1:10:52–1:18:15)
- **His second success was golf, not supplements.** He partnered with his then-girlfriend's father, a retired tour pro with 40 years designing club heads, on a direct-to-consumer wedge brand. Product development cost about £50–60k. It launched in 2019 and reached **six figures a month within about 6 months**, helped by COVID, when golf was one of the few things allowed and people had more spare money.
- **The influencer approach.** He sent wedges to big golf YouTubers **without paying them and without scripting**: "if it doesn't work, tell everyone." He says it worked because creators had less content then and influencer marketing wasn't yet crowded.
- **Lesson on partnerships.** Pairing a product expert with someone who can sell online is a shortcut.
- **Supply chain.** Manufacturers prioritise big repeat orders. Small brands are last in line for components, which capped growth.

### His framework and offer (1:20:07–1:25:00, 2:08:04 to end)
- **One repeatable seven-step framework.** He says he used the same process for the nutrition brand, two golf brands, a Taiwanese golf ball maker and a Scottish brand. He says the Scottish brand went from **£20–30k a month to six-figure months in 3 months**, and has just done its first profitable seven-figure year. He offers to "show the screenshots".
- **Clients who insist "we're different"** are the ones it doesn't work for, in his view.
- **His offer in 2024:** free one-hour store teardown calls, posted on YouTube. Brands at £5–10k a month could hire him for about 2 one-to-one calls a month plus a course built on the seven-step framework. A higher done-for-you tier was run by his team. This looks like the forerunner of Supplement Brand's current model.

### Inconsistencies to keep in mind
- The description claims "$10M+ in online sales" and "£10k startup into a six-figure success". The transcript gives monthly figures but no audited totals.
- The nutrition brand's revenue moves between "40–60k a month" in the same conversation.
- Nothing in the video mentions Dragons' Den or Shopify Masters, which the agency website claims.

## What this means for us

1. **Niche down hard.** "Sports supplement" on its own is broader than any of their winners. Their pattern is one person, one problem and one hero product.
2. **Creatine in a new format is crowded.** Gummies, honey, coffee and electrolyte blends are all already in their portfolio. A collagen jelly snack fits the "familiar ingredient, new format" pattern, but we'd need a clear avatar, like the recovery angle for athletes that ODED uses.
3. **Model the money at a $40–80 price point** with 2 and 3-pack breaks, subscribe and save, and a 60 to 90-day guarantee. Use their profit calculator before the call if possible.
4. **Validate before building.** Lou's own first launch took two years and made one sale. His advice is to test demand and collect objections from real buyers first, for example by testing the jelly snack idea with a pre-order or survey before paying for a custom run.
5. **Several "wins" started on white-label** and moved to a custom formula once they were selling (Meltem, and possibly Camden). Ask whether that's their default path.

## Questions for Wednesday

**About the offer**
1. What does the paid programme cost and what's included? Is it coaching, done-for-you, or both? Is it a one-off fee, monthly, or revenue share?
2. How long is the commitment, and what support continues after launch?

**About the results**
3. How many brands have launched through you in total, not just these 33? What share reach $10k a month, and how fast?
4. Are the quoted figures revenue or profit? What ad spend and ROAS sit behind Meltem's $90k and Crushs' $5k a day?
5. Can we see a Shopify or ads dashboard for one or two of these brands, or speak to a founder directly?

**About cash and economics**
6. What's a realistic all-in cash requirement to first sale: stock and MOQs, design, photos, the site, and a test ad budget?
7. What target COGS, CAC, contribution margin and LTV do you plan around? What subscription retention do clients actually see?
8. How do refunds under the long guarantees affect margin?

**About the product**
9. White-label or custom formula first? Which manufacturers do you use, what are typical MOQs, and who owns the formula and supplier relationship?
10. How would you assess a collagen jelly snack: manufacturing, shelf life, minimums and cost per unit? Versus a sports product like ODED or Pumpy?
11. How do you choose a niche, and how would you narrow "sports supplement" into a single avatar?

**About risk**
12. How do you handle claims compliance and labelling (FDA, EFSA, TGA, Health Canada)? Several client pages make strong health claims.
13. Which channel drives launches: Meta ads, TikTok creators, or influencers? What happens when organic stalls, as in Finn's story?
14. What do you see go wrong most often in launches that fail?

**From the video**
15. You started on £29.30 retail against £17–18 cost. What gross margin and maximum acquisition cost do you make clients hit before they order stock?
16. You say "validate it, then build it". What exactly do we have to validate before committing to a production run, and how?
17. What are the seven steps, and which of them does the paid programme do for us versus teach us?
18. Is Youth Sport Nutrition still trading, and what does it do a month now? Can we see the Scottish brand's screenshots you mentioned?
19. Do you still use gifted, unscripted creator seeding, or is it paid UGC and Meta ads now that creator marketing is crowded?
20. Do you help with manufacturer introductions? You phoned 52 to find one. And can you help structure early funding, like your staged investor deal?
