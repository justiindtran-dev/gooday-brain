# Gooday Nutritionist Review: 22 November 2026

**General information only, not medical advice.** This review looks at the *target* product spec, not lab results. Every marketing claim drawn from it still has to pass the FSANZ claims rules (Food Standards Code Standard 1.2.7, with Schedules 4 and 23), and the regulatory consultant must sign it off before use. People who are pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication, or managing a medical condition should check with their GP, pharmacist or an Accredited Practising Dietitian (APD).

Format: one 120 g squeeze pouch a day.

---

## 1. Ingredient by ingredient

### 1.1 Hydrolysed collagen peptides, 5 g (Angle A)
- **What it does in the body:** Collagen is digested into amino acids (mostly glycine, proline and hydroxyproline) and some small di- and tripeptides, such as Pro-Hyp, that reach the blood. Lab studies suggest these peptides may signal skin and cartilage cells. Your body does not route eaten collagen straight to your skin.
- **Evidence for skin:**
  - Industry-heavy meta-analyses report better skin hydration and elasticity. Example: Pu et al. 2023, *Nutrients*, 26 RCTs ([PubMed 37432180](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432180/)).
  - A 2025 meta-analysis split the trials by funding and quality. Industry-funded and low-quality trials showed benefits. Independent and high-quality trials showed **no significant effect** on wrinkles, elasticity or hydration ([PubMed 40761858](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40761858/); coverage: [NutraIngredients](https://nutraingredients.com/Article/2025/08/26/industry-reacts-to-meta-analysis-concluding-collagen-supplements-show-no-proven-benefit-for-skin-aging), [ScienceAlert](https://www.sciencealert.com/scientists-find-major-flaws-in-the-research-behind-collagen-supplements)).
  - A 2025 review of dietary interventions for skin ageing is here: [PubMed 41174715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41174715/).
  - **Rating: low to moderate certainty, and contested.**
- **Evidence for joints:**
  - Collagen gave a small improvement in osteoarthritis pain and stiffness scores (García-Coronado et al. 2019, *Int Orthop* ([PubMed 30368550](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30368550/))). Collagen peptide in knee OA: [PubMed 37717022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37717022/).
  - The trials are small and short, and most doses were about 10 g a day.
  - **Rating: low certainty, small effect.**
- **Evidence for bone:** One RCT gave 5 g of specific collagen peptides to postmenopausal women and found modest bone mineral density gains (König et al. 2018, *Nutrients*). It is a single trial using one branded peptide, so the result should not be applied to collagen in general.
- **Is 5 g meaningful?**
  - It is at the low end of the skin trials, which used 2.5 to 10 g. Results depend on the specific peptide: Verisol, Peptan and Fortigel each have their own trials.
  - It is below the roughly 10 g used in most joint trials.
  - As protein, 5 g is about 7 to 10% of an adult's daily needs. Collagen is an **incomplete protein**: it lacks tryptophan, so its PDCAAS is about 0. It is poor for satiety or muscle building compared with dairy or soy protein.
- **Downsides:**
  - Generally very well tolerated. Occasional fullness, heartburn or an aftertaste.
  - **Marine collagen means a fish allergen.** Fish must be declared under PEAL labelling. It is also unsuitable for anyone with a fish allergy.
  - **Bovine collagen** is not vegetarian, may not be halal or kosher unless certified, and needs BSE-safe sourcing documents.
  - Contaminant risk: heavy metals (marine sources) and quality variability. Ask suppliers for a lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic certificate of analysis on every batch.
  - Kidney disease: total protein matters, but 5 g is small. People on a protein restriction should check with their renal team.
  - No known meaningful drug interactions.

### 1.2 Vitamin C, at least 40 mg, from Kakadu plum or acerola (both products)
- **What it does:** It is an essential cofactor for making collagen (prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase). It is also an antioxidant, supports immune function, and improves iron absorption from plant foods when eaten with them.
- **Evidence:** Strong for its essential roles. Deficiency causes scurvy, which features collagen breakdown. There is **no good evidence** that extra vitamin C makes eaten collagen work better in people who already get enough. The "collagen + vitamin C" pairing makes sense on paper but has not been shown to add benefit.
- **Is the dose meaningful?** Yes. It is roughly the daily requirement: the Australian labelling RDI is 40 mg, and the adult RDI is 45 mg. Most Australians already reach this through fruit and vegetables, though.
- **Downsides:**
  - Kidney stones: the risk link is with **high-dose supplements**, about 1,000 mg a day (Thomas et al. 2013, *JAMA Intern Med*). 40 to 100 mg is not a concern. People with a history of oxalate stones should still count total intake across supplements.
  - Haemochromatosis: vitamin C increases iron absorption. This is a minor issue at this dose, but worth a note for that group.
- **Regulatory notes:**
  - Vitamin C occurs naturally in Kakadu plum, so it is not an "added vitamin" under Standard 1.3.2. Claims still need the RDI thresholds: "source of" needs at least 10% RDI per serve, and "good source" needs at least 25%.
  - Vitamin C is **heat- and oxygen-sensitive**. Pasteurisation and shelf life will lower it. Claim the **end-of-shelf-life** value, which may mean adding extra at the start (overage).

### 1.3 Fibre, 6 g: resistant dextrin or soluble corn fibre, plus apple pectin (Angle B)
- **What it does:**
  - Resistant dextrin (for example Nutriose) and soluble corn fibre (for example Promitor) are soluble, slowly fermented fibres. They feed gut bacteria and produce short-chain fatty acids. They modestly blunt blood glucose rises and add a little stool bulk.
  - Pectin forms a gel, which can slow stomach emptying and lower LDL cholesterol a little. That effect needs gram-level daily doses, roughly 6 g or more of pectin itself.
- **Evidence:**
  - Resistant dextrin for weight: a meta-analysis of RCTs found small reductions in body weight, BMI and fat mass in overweight adults ([PMC5434557](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5434557/)). Trials are mostly small, many are from Asia, and many use higher doses (10 to 30 g).
  - Resistant dextrin for glucose and lipids: newer reviews report modest improvements ([BMC Nutr 2025](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s40795-025-01080-8); [Front Nutr 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1655664/full)). **Rating: low to moderate certainty.**
  - Satiety: in a systematic review, only a minority of fibre treatments actually increased satiety, and results were inconsistent (Clark & Slavin 2013, *J Am Coll Nutr*). Viscous fibres such as psyllium, beta-glucan and guar do best. Low-viscosity dextrins do less.
  - Fibre in general: higher total fibre intake is strongly linked to lower risk of death, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer (Reynolds et al. 2019, *Lancet*). The benefit is mainly seen at 25 to 29 g a day or more, mostly from whole foods.
- **Is 6 g meaningful?**
  - It is about 20 to 24% of the adult Adequate Intake (25 g for women, 30 g for men).
  - The average Australian eats about 20 to 23 g, so 6 g closes much of the gap.
  - It qualifies for "**good source of dietary fibre**" (at least 4 g per serve, FSANZ Schedule 4) but not "**excellent source**" (at least 7 g) ([Schedule 4](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2015L00474/2024-10-29/2024-10-29/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)).
  - It is an isolated fibre. It does not bring the polyphenols, structure and variety of fibre from vegetables, legumes and whole grains.
- **Downsides:**
  - **Gas, bloating and loose stools**, especially if fibre intake rises suddenly. At 6 g these are usually mild, and resistant dextrin is one of the better-tolerated fibres. Advise people to drink water and build up over a week.
  - **FODMAPs:**
    - Resistant dextrin and soluble corn fibre are generally tolerated in moderate doses. They are not inulin or FOS, which are high-FODMAP.
    - Purified apple pectin is not the same as apple, which is high in fructose and sorbitol. Even so, ask the supplier whether it is certified or tested low-FODMAP.
    - The one real FODMAP risk across the range is the **erythritol** (a polyol). Get **Monash University low-FODMAP certification** before making any gut-sensitive claim.
  - **Medication timing:** soluble fibre can reduce or delay absorption of some medicines. Examples are levothyroxine, digoxin, some antidepressants, metformin (it changes glucose response), and the oral contraceptive pill, which is less certain. A practical rule is to take medications 2 hours apart from the pouch.
  - **Diabetes and insulin users:** possible small effects on blood glucose. This is a benefit, but worth knowing.

### 1.4 Greens powder, about 2 g: spinach, kale, wheatgrass (Angle B)
- **What it does:** It is dried, powdered leafy greens. It carries small amounts of folate, vitamin K, carotenoids, polyphenols and nitrate.
- **Evidence:** There are **no robust RCTs** showing health outcomes from greens powders at any dose. Most studies are small, industry-funded, and test proprietary blends at much higher doses (10 g or more). Wheatgrass's claims, such as detox and alkalising, are not supported. **Rating: very low certainty.**
- **Is 2 g meaningful?** **No.** Dried leafy greens are about 8 to 12 times concentrated. So 2 g of powder equals roughly 15 to 25 g of fresh greens. One Australian Dietary Guidelines serve of vegetables is 75 g, or 1 cup of raw salad leaves. **This is about a quarter of one serve, or less**, against a target of 5 to 6 serves a day. It cannot be marketed as replacing vegetables.
- **Downsides:**
  - **Vitamin K and warfarin:** the amount is small, but kale and spinach are rich in vitamin K. People on warfarin need a *consistent* vitamin K intake, so a daily pouch is fine if it is taken consistently and their INR is checked. Tell them to let their GP know. This does not affect the newer blood thinners (DOACs).
  - **Oxalate (spinach):** trivial at 2 g, but relevant if the dose is raised.
  - **Heavy metals and pesticides:** greens powders are a known category risk for lead and cadmium. Test every batch.
  - **Wheatgrass and gluten:** pure wheatgrass has no gluten, but cross-contamination with wheat seed is common. Either do not claim "gluten-free", or test to "no detectable gluten" (the Australian standard is very strict).
  - **Microbial safety:** dried raw greens need a validated kill step, which the pouch pasteurisation probably provides. Confirm this with the co-packer.

### 1.5 Sweeteners: stevia or monk fruit, plus erythritol (both products)
- **Stevia (steviol glycosides):** Well studied and permitted. The ADI is 4 mg/kg/day as steviol equivalents, and one pouch is far below that. Some people dislike the bitter aftertaste. Effects on the gut microbiome are uncertain and have not been shown to matter in humans.
- **Monk fruit (luo han guo):** Little human research, but no safety signals. **The consultant must confirm its exact permitted form and labelling in Australia** before launch.
- **Erythritol:** a sugar alcohol that is about 90% absorbed and passed in urine. It is calorie-free and does not raise blood glucose.
  - **GI effects:** less laxative than sorbitol or maltitol. Even so, large single doses (above about 0.5 g/kg, or 25 to 35 g for an adult) cause nausea, gas and diarrhoea. Sensitive people and IBS sufferers react at much lower doses, and it **stacks with the fibre in Angle B**.
  - **Cardiovascular signal:**
    - Witkowski et al. 2023 (*Nature Medicine*) found that higher blood erythritol was linked with higher risk of heart attack, stroke and death in at-risk US and European cohorts ([PMC10275890](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10275890/)). In lab and human tests, erythritol increased platelet clotting.
    - A 2024 follow-up (*ATVB*) gave healthy adults a 30 g erythritol drink. It made platelets more reactive than glucose did ([Healio summary](https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20240808/sugar-substitute-erythritol-linked-to-elevated-risk-for-blood-clot-formation); [Cleveland Clinic](https://consultqd.clevelandclinic.org/evidence-mounts-that-sugar-substitute-erythritol-raises-cardiovascular-risk)).
    - **Caveats:** the 2023 link is observational. The body also makes erythritol itself, so high blood levels may be a *marker* of metabolic disease rather than a cause. The 30 g dose is far above what one pouch would contain, which is probably a few grams.
    - **Honest summary:** not proven harmful, but there is an unresolved safety signal, and it attracts media attention. It is a reputational risk for a "wellness" brand, especially with older or cardiac-risk buyers.
  - **Alternative:** **allulose (D-allulose) is now an approved novel food in Australia and New Zealand** (FSANZ A1247, gazetted in 2024 ([FSANZ](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/food-standards-code/applications/A1247-D-allulose-as-a-novel-food))). Check its permitted uses and labelling conditions with the consultant.

### 1.6 Gelling agents: gelatin, pectin, gellan
- These are used in small amounts and are low risk.
- **Gelatin** is animal-derived: not vegetarian or vegan, and halal or kosher only if certified. Marine gelatin has the same fish allergen issue as marine collagen.
- **Gellan and pectin** are vegan and well tolerated.

## 2. Product reviews

### Angle A: Collagen (peach; raspberry lychee)
**Per pouch:** 5 g hydrolysed collagen, at least 40 mg vitamin C, about 35 kcal, under 2 g sugar.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Best-supported benefit** | Meets the daily vitamin C requirement. Low sugar and low kilojoule. Easy to take. |
| **Plausible but uncertain** | Small improvements in skin hydration and elasticity. Independent high-quality trials show no clear effect. |
| **Weak at this dose** | Joint pain relief: most trials used about 10 g. |
| **Not supported** | Wrinkle reduction, hair or nail growth, "anti-ageing", gut lining or "leaky gut" repair. |
| **Protein value** | 5 g of incomplete protein. Do not position it as a protein snack. |

**Who it suits:** Adults who want a convenient, low-sugar daily habit and accept that the skin benefit is uncertain. Active adults or older people with joint niggles may get some benefit, and are better served at 10 g. People with low fruit intake get useful vitamin C.

**Who should avoid it or check first:**
- **Fish allergy** (marine version) and anyone with animal-product, vegetarian, halal or kosher needs (bovine collagen or gelatin, unless certified).
- People with **kidney disease** on a protein restriction.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** collagen is a food protein and is likely fine, but there are no specific safety trials. Talk to a GP or midwife, and do not market to this group.
- **Under-18s:** no need and no evidence. Do not target them.
- Anyone sensitive to erythritol, and people with high cardiovascular risk who want to avoid erythritol for now.

**How a dietitian would honestly describe it:** "It's a low-sugar snack with your vitamin C for the day and a modest dose of collagen. Collagen is safe, but the skin evidence is mostly from industry-funded studies, and the independent ones don't show much. If you enjoy it, there's little harm. Don't expect visible results, and don't count it as a meaningful protein source."

**Formulation suggestions:**
1. **Use a named, trial-backed peptide at its studied dose.** Examples: Verisol at 2.5 g for skin, Fortigel at 5 g for joints, or Peptan at 5 to 10 g. Or move to **10 g** to match most skin and joint trials. This also makes any future claim easier to substantiate.
2. **Add 5 to 10 g of complete protein** (whey or milk protein isolate, or pea or soy for vegan) to make it a genuine protein snack. At 10 g or more total protein it can make a "source of protein" claim. Check the Schedule 4 conditions, including the protein quality rules that may limit how much collagen counts.
3. **Replace or cut erythritol.** Use stevia or monk fruit alone, or allulose (now permitted in Australia and New Zealand). This removes the GI and cardiovascular questions.
4. **Default to bovine collagen with a pectin gel**, and keep marine as a clearly labelled pescatarian SKU. This shrinks the allergen footprint and makes the product halal-certifiable.
5. **Claim vitamin C at end-of-shelf-life levels.** Lean on the permitted vitamin C claim for normal collagen formation and connective tissue, if it is in Schedule 23 and the consultant confirms it. Do not make skin claims for collagen itself.

**Bottom line (Angle A):** A safe, low-sugar, convenient pouch that reliably delivers a day's vitamin C. Its headline ingredient, collagen, has contested evidence for skin and only weak evidence for joints at this 5 g dose. It is a "nice to have", not a proven skin treatment. The biggest improvements would be a trial-backed peptide at its studied dose (or 10 g), some complete protein, and dropping erythritol. Marketing must stay within the vitamin C and permitted claims, and avoid "anti-ageing" or skin-result promises.

### Angle B: Fibre and greens (green apple; pink grapefruit)
**Per pouch:** 6 g fibre, about 2 g greens powder, at least 40 mg vitamin C, about 25 kcal, under 2 g sugar.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Best-supported benefit** | 6 g of soluble fibre, about 20 to 24% of the daily target. Supports regularity and gut bacteria fermentation. Meets the daily vitamin C requirement. |
| **Plausible, modest** | Slightly smaller blood glucose rises. Small effects on weight and cholesterol with long-term use (low certainty, often at higher doses). |
| **Weak** | Satiety: low-viscosity fibres are inconsistent. |
| **Not supported** | Greens powder as a vegetable replacement, "detox", "alkalising", "superfood" claims. 2 g is under a quarter of one vegetable serve. |

**Who it suits:** Adults who eat too little fibre, which is most Australians, and want a low-sugar, low-kilojoule top-up. It may help people managing their weight or blood glucose as part of an overall diet.

**Who should avoid it or check first:**
- **IBS or a sensitive gut:** start with half a pouch. Check FODMAP certification, and note that erythritol plus fibre may trigger symptoms.
- **People on warfarin:** take it consistently and tell your GP. Vitamin K content is small but present.
- **People on levothyroxine, digoxin or other timing-sensitive medicines:** take them 2 hours apart.
- **Coeliac disease:** check gluten status, because of possible wheatgrass contamination.
- **Grapefruit flavour:** if the pink grapefruit SKU contains real grapefruit juice or peel, it **may interact with medicines** metabolised by CYP3A4. Examples are some statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin), calcium channel blockers, some immunosuppressants and some anti-anxiety drugs. Confirm whether the flavour is natural grapefruit or a flavour system. If real grapefruit is present, add a pharmacist warning or reformulate. **This is the most important safety check in the range.**
- People with **swallowing difficulties or bowel strictures** should check with their doctor before using fibre supplements.
- **Pregnancy:** fibre is helpful, since constipation is common. Greens and wheatgrass have no specific safety data at this dose, and microbial safety matters. Talk to a GP or midwife.
- **Under-18s:** not needed. Do not target them.

**How a dietitian would honestly describe it:** "It's a decent, low-sugar way to get about a fifth of your daily fibre, using well-tolerated fibres. The 'greens' part is mostly a label: it's less than a quarter of a serve of vegetables, so it doesn't replace eating them. Build up slowly, drink water, and space it from your medications."

**Formulation suggestions:**
1. **Raise fibre to at least 7 g** to qualify for "excellent source of dietary fibre" (Schedule 4). Ideally go to 8 to 10 g, split across two textures so it stays tolerable.
2. **Add a viscous, evidence-backed fibre.** Candidates:
   - **Psyllium**, which has strong RCT evidence for LDL cholesterol, glycaemic control and bowel regularity.
   - **Partially hydrolysed guar gum (PHGG)**, which is low-FODMAP and has IBS evidence.
   - **Oat or barley beta-glucan**, which is linked to an FSANZ *high-level* health claim on blood cholesterol (needs 3 g a day, at least 1 g per serve). This could be a real, claimable point of difference.
3. **Make the greens honest:**
   - Raise them to about 5 to 10 g of powder (closer to a meaningful vegetable contribution), or
   - Drop the "greens" headline and position the product on fibre, or
   - Swap to a standardised vegetable or fruit polyphenol extract with its own evidence.
   - In every case, test for heavy metals and gluten.
4. **Remove erythritol** in favour of stevia or monk fruit alone, or allulose. Stacking a polyol on top of fermentable fibre is the most likely cause of bloating complaints. Then pursue **Monash low-FODMAP certification**.
5. **Consider 5 to 10 g of protein** (pea or whey). It would improve satiety, which fibre alone does inconsistently, and make the product more of a snack. Also confirm there is **no real grapefruit** in the pink grapefruit SKU, or label it accordingly.

**Bottom line (Angle B):** The fibre is the real value: 6 g of well-tolerated soluble fibre is a meaningful, evidence-backed top-up for most Australians, who fall short on fibre. The vitamin C is a solid bonus. The greens are too small a dose to matter, so do not let marketing imply vegetable replacement or detox. The main risks are gut symptoms (fibre plus erythritol), medication timing, warfarin consistency, and a possible grapefruit-drug interaction. Pushing fibre to 7 g or more with a viscous fibre like psyllium or beta-glucan, and dropping erythritol, would turn a good product into a claimable, clearly differentiated one.

## 3. Claims guardrails (for the regulatory consultant)
- **Probably available, subject to confirmation:**
  - "Good source of dietary fibre" (Angle B, at least 4 g per serve).
  - "Excellent source" only at 7 g or more.
  - "Good source of vitamin C" (both products, at end-of-shelf-life levels).
  - "Low sugar", if under 2.5 g/100 mL for liquids or under 5 g/100 g for solids. Check which applies to a gel pouch.
  - Vitamin C general-level health claims that are in Schedule 23.
- **Not available without self-substantiation and notification to FSANZ:** any collagen skin, joint, hair or nail claim; greens claims; satiety or weight claims for resistant dextrin.
- **Never:** therapeutic or disease claims ("treats arthritis", "reverses ageing", "detox", "heals the gut"). These risk the product being classed as a therapeutic good under the TGA.
- **Mandatory declarations:** fish (marine collagen or gelatin), and wheat or gluten if wheatgrass is not tested clean. Also check whether the polyol excess-consumption statement applies at the erythritol dose used.
